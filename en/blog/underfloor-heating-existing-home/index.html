Underfloor heating in a home you already live in: how it is done and how long it takes

The question we hear most from people who already live in the house is not whether underfloor heating works. It is whether the floor has to come up, how much higher it ends up and how many weeks the house is a building site. The answer depends on two measurements we take on the first visit: how much height there is to spare, and what floor is there today.

Published: 13 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 10 min · Author: iStarTec

Can I add underfloor heating to a home I already live in?

Yes, and without moving out if the work is done one area at a time. There are two ways: lift the floor and lay the classic system, with insulation, pipe and a new screed, or bond a low-profile system on top of the existing floor, which raises it by about 2 cm plus the new finish. The choice depends on the height available at doors and steps and on the condition of the current floor. iStarTec installs Uponor underfloor heating and connects it to a Bosch heat pump.

  • Classic system: insulation + pipe + screed; the existing floor almost always has to come up
  • Low-profile: 12 mm panel with 9.9 mm pipe, covered with a thin self-levelling compound, laid over the existing floor
  • Time on site: allow 2 to 3 weeks per area with the classic system, most of it drying time; less with low-profile
  • Floor finish: tiles and stone work best; vinyl and wood only with a compatible data sheet
  • Permit: usually not needed for interior work that does not touch the structure (RJUE, art. 6)
  • Quote: free, after a technical visit, with a reply within 24 working hours

Two ways to add underfloor heating to an existing house

In a new build, underfloor heating goes in before the screed and nobody ever sees it. In a finished house you have to find room for the pipe, and there are two ways to do it.

Classic systemLow-profile system
What is doneThe floor is lifted; insulation, pipe and a new screed go inA thin panel is bonded to the existing floor, the pipe clips into it, and it is covered with self-levelling compound
Height neededSeveral centimetres for insulation, pipe and screedAbout 2 cm, plus the final floor finish
Existing floorComes upStays, if it is sound and level
Insulation underneathYes, as thick as the height allowsLittle or none, which matters on the ground floor
Response when switched onSlow: it heats a lot of mass and stores the heatFaster: there is less mass to heat
Best forRenovations where the floor is coming up anywayHomes where you do not want to break the floor, or where there is no height

Low-profile example: Uponor Minitec, with a 12 mm panel and 9.9 mm PE-Xa pipe. There are also dry systems, such as Uponor Siccus Mini, that need no wet compound over the pipe.

If the floor is being replaced anyway, the classic system is usually the better option: it gets insulation underneath, which cuts the heat lost into the ground or to the floor below. If the floor is in good condition and you would rather not break it up, a low-profile system does the job with far less dust and fewer days on site.

Height decides almost everything

The first thing we measure on the visit is how much height the house can give. Every centimetre the floor rises has to fit in four places:

  • Interior doors. A door can be trimmed at the bottom, but only so far. Pocket doors and glass doors are harder.
  • Stairs. If the landing rises and the step does not, the first or last step ends up a different height from the rest. That is the kind of detail people trip on.
  • Doors to the outside. On a terrace or garden door, the threshold must stay above the outside level so rainwater does not come in.
  • Things that sit on the floor. Shower trays, toilets, skirting boards and fitted kitchen units.

In many Algarve villas with old ceramic tiles, the cleanest solution is to lift the tiles and the bedding layer under them. That frees the height the classic system needs, and the finished floor ends up where it was. When there is bare slab underneath and no margin at all, low-profile becomes the only option.

How long it takes, stage by stage

The pipe goes in quickly. What makes the job take time is drying. For one area of the house, such as a living room with an open kitchen, the classic system goes in this order:

  1. Lifting the floor and cleaning the base: 2 to 4 days, depending on the floor and the layer underneath.
  2. Insulation, edge strip, pipe and manifold: 1 to 2 days.
  3. Pressure test of the pipe, which stays under pressure while the screed is laid, so any damage shows up straight away.
  4. Screed: 1 day.
  5. Drying: allow about 3 weeks for a cement screed before the first heat-up, and about 1 week for an anhydrite self-levelling screed. The screed manufacturer's data sheet always has the final say.
  6. First heat-up, with the temperature raised gradually over several days, as EN 1264-4 requires. It helps dry the screed and prevents cracking.
  7. Floor finish, once the screed is dry.

In total, allow 2 to 3 weeks per area, most of it drying time. During that time the floor can be walked on carefully and the rest of the house works as normal. With low-profile, the thin compound dries in a few days and the finish goes on much sooner.

Do not rush the first heat-up

Running the underfloor heating flat out to dry the screed faster is the quickest way to crack it. Start-up is done at low temperatures that rise step by step, which is why it is part of our job and not left to the owner.

How the work goes with the family at home

Most families we work with stay in the house during the work. This is how:

  • One area at a time. First the living room, then the bedrooms, or one floor at a time. Furniture moves to the part of the house that is not being worked on.
  • Dust kept in. Lifting tiles is dusty. We seal off the area with plastic sheeting, protect the rest of the floor and clean up at the end of each day.
  • Water and power stay on. Underfloor heating does not touch the house's water supply. There are only short interruptions when the manifold is connected to the heat source.
  • Wireless thermostats. Each room gets its own thermostat, and with wireless models there is no need to cut channels in the walls for cables.
  • A place for the manifold. The manifold feeds the circuits and sits in a recessed or wall-mounted cabinet, near the middle of the house. A wardrobe or a corridor usually works.

If the underfloor heating comes with other work, such as replumbing or new bathrooms, it is worth doing it all in the same phase. The floor only gets opened once. We explain replumbing in our guide to replumbing an old house in Portugal.

Which floor finishes work on top

The floor finish is the last layer the heat has to cross. The better it lets heat through, the lower the water temperature can be and the more efficiently the heat pump runs.

  • Ceramic, porcelain and stone: the best choice. They conduct heat well and are not affected by it.
  • Vinyl: works, as long as the manufacturer states it is suitable for underfloor heating and gives a maximum surface temperature.
  • Engineered wood: works with products made for it, thin and glued down. Thick solid wood insulates too much and moves with the heat.
  • Carpet and thick rugs: avoid. A large rug in the living room takes output away from the area it covers.

As a guide, manufacturers recommend floor finishes with a thermal resistance of up to about 0.15 m²·K/W. The value is on the product data sheet; if the shop cannot tell you, ask for the sheet before you buy.

Where the heat comes from

Underfloor heating runs on warm water, usually between 30 and 40 °C. That is where an air-to-water heat pump is most efficient, so the two almost always go together. The same unit can heat the house in winter, cool the floor in summer and make the domestic hot water.

  • If the house has no heat pump yet, we size one for the underfloor heating and the hot water. We work with Bosch.
  • If it already has an air-to-water heat pump, we check that its output and operating temperature suit the underfloor heating.
  • If the house has a gas boiler: iStarTec does not do gas work. In that case the usual route is to use the project to switch to a heat pump.

Do you need a permit?

Usually not. Article 6 of Portugal's planning and building code (RJUE, Decree-Law 555/99) exempts maintenance work and interior alterations that do not touch the structure, the façades or the roof. Replacing the floor and adding underfloor heating falls under this in most homes.

In an apartment, read the condominium rules first. Some require notice for noisy work and set working hours.

When we do not recommend it

Underfloor heating is the most comfortable way to heat a house, but it is not the answer for every one. We will tell you on the visit if your house falls into one of these cases:

  • Houses used only at weekends in winter. The classic system takes hours to warm the house. If you arrive on Friday night and leave on Sunday, air conditioning or a fast-response system works better, unless the underfloor heating is kept on at a low temperature and controlled remotely.
  • Floors you do not want to lose and no height for low-profile, such as valuable old floorboards.
  • Houses with no insulation at all in the walls and roof. Underfloor heating will heat them, but a large share of the heat is lost. Sometimes it pays to start with insulation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to move out during the work?

In most cases, no. The work is done one area at a time, furniture moves to the part of the house that is not being worked on, and water and power stay on. Only a small house done all at once means moving out for a few days.

How much does underfloor heating raise the floor?

With a low-profile system, about 2 cm plus the final finish. The classic system needs several centimetres, so the old floor and the layer under it are normally lifted and the finished floor stays at the same height.

Can I put underfloor heating in just one room?

Yes. Starting with the living room or a bathroom is common. It is worth planning the manifold and the heat source with capacity for the other rooms from the start, so nothing has to be redone when you extend it.

How long does the work take?

Allow 2 to 3 weeks per area with the classic system. Laying the pipe takes 1 to 2 days; the rest is screed drying and the first heat-up, done with temperatures raised step by step. With low-profile, the thin compound dries in a few days.

Does underfloor heating work under a wooden floor?

It works with engineered wood made for underfloor heating, thin and glued down. Thick solid wood insulates too much. Check on the data sheet that the floor is compatible and what its thermal resistance is; the usual guide is up to about 0.15 m²·K/W.

Do I need planning permission?

Usually not. Interior alterations that do not touch the structure, the façades or the roof are exempt under article 6 of the RJUE (Decree-Law 555/99). In an apartment, also check the condominium rules.
